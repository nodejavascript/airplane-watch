Watch a named aircraft leave a named airport

Pick an airport, point a fence at it, and name the aircraft you care about by tail number or callsign. The page reads a public flight feed every ten seconds and tells you the moment one of yours is airborne — with a browser notification if you want one. Everything runs in this tab. There is no account, no key, and nothing to install.

  • It is honest about the hard part. An aircraft sitting on the apron is only heard by a receiver a few kilometres away, so a departure is usually detected seconds after it is airborne, not at the moment of rotation. Every row says which of the two you got, in its phase column.
  • Your watchlist stays in your browser. Tail numbers you type are matched locally and never sent here or to Google.
  • Free and open data. The feed is adsb.lol, published under the Open Database License.

    The two airports are looked up, not heard — and the times are this page's own. An aircraft broadcasts who it is, never where it came from or where it is going, so both airport codes come from a separate free lookup on the callsign — which means a diversion, a reassigned callsign or an out-of-date record can make them wrong. A dash means no route is on file for that callsign, and “asking…” means the lookup has not answered yet. The time under either airport is not from the feed either, and it says which it is: took off means this page watched that aircraft leave the ground, while first seen means it was already flying when the page opened — the feed never sends a takeoff time at all. The in about … under the destination is an estimate, not an arrival time: it is the straight-line distance still to run at the speed the aircraft reports over the ground, and it assumes it flies that line at that speed all the way. Every other column is what the feed itself reported, taken at the time shown.

    What this cannot see — read this before you rely on it

    • It cannot tell you the wheels are leaving the ground. The feed reports where an aircraft is, not that it has gone. What this page can say is that something it had on the ground is now airborne — and the usual case is a departure noticed a few seconds late, because surface coverage from a far-off receiver is poor. An aircraft that is never heard while it is low is never seen to leave at all. That is why the departure column shows a time only when this page watched it happen — and why most rows, whose aeroplanes were already flying when the page opened, carry no time there at all rather than one that would mean nothing.
    • Only aircraft that broadcast their own position appear. One that has asked not to be published, or an older one that sends only its identity, is either absent or arrives with no position at all. Missing from this page is not the same as not flying.
    • A missing altitude is not an aircraft on the ground. Some transponders send a position and no altitude. Those readings are shown as no altitude, and they are never used to decide that something has left the ground.
    • A flyover is not a departure. An aircraft already airborne when it enters your fence is ignored, however low it is. That is deliberate, and this is what it costs: a real departure from a nearby airfield, climbing through your fence, is not reported.
    • The fence is a circle, and reception is not. How far a receiver reaches depends on the aircraft's height, the terrain and the weather, so this page shows what one network could hear, not what is up there. Distances are in kilometres; the feed works in nautical miles, one of which is 1.852 km, and the conversion is exact.
    • The feed will refuse you, and the page says so instead of hiding it. Ask too often and the answer is a refusal, HTTP 429. So the page slows itself: it looks every 20 seconds at first, never more often than every 15, doubles the gap after a refusal up to three minutes, recovers on its own, and stops altogether while the tab is in the background. A proxy in front of the feed caches for a few seconds, so a crowd does not multiply the load. Measured on 20 September 2026: ten requests three seconds apart were refused from the third or fourth onwards.
    • Positions are approximate, and not quite now. The feed updates in seconds, and a position worked out from several receivers is coarser than one the aircraft reported itself.

    How it works

    1. Where the position comes from. Aircraft broadcast who they are, where they are and how high they are, on 1090 MHz. Volunteers with a cheap receiver and an antenna hear those broadcasts and send them to adsb.lol, which republishes the merged picture for anyone to read.
    2. Why there is a proxy in the middle. The feed sends no cross-origin headers, so a browser is not allowed to read it directly — the request goes out, the answer comes back, and the browser discards it. Every call this page makes therefore goes to /api/… on this domain, and a small proxy passes it on and adds the header the browser needs. The map squares and the photographs of each type are fetched the same way, so this page asks no other site for anything.
    3. How a departure is decided. Every twenty seconds at first — more slowly if the feed refuses, and not at all while the tab is in the background — the page asks for every aircraft inside the distance you chose. Each reading is classified as on the ground, airborne, or unknown, and compared with the same aircraft's previous reading. Ground to airborne inside your fence is a departure; seen only once it is already climbing is a probable one; already airborne is ignored; a reading too old to compare proves nothing. A departure is what can set off an alert — but only for something on your list, a type you starred or a tail number you named.
    4. Where it is kept. Nowhere on a server. Your watchlist, the place you gave, the distance you set, the filters you chose and the tails you highlighted live in this browser's own storage, and go when you clear this site's data.

    Privacy

    This policy governs aircraft-demo.nodejavascript.com, and it is written from what the code does rather than from a template. It was last changed on 22 September 2026.

    • There is no account and no server-side store. Your watchlist, the place you gave, the airport you aimed the fence at, the distance you set, the filters you chose and the tails you highlighted are kept in your own browser's storage. Two things do leave your browser, and only so they can be looked up: a place name you search for, and the callsigns of the aircraft inside your fence, which are looked up to find where they came from and where they are going. Neither is written down by this site.
    • Google Analytics loads only after you say yes. Google's script is not in the page. It is added by consent.js when you press Accept all, and if you press Reject all then no request is made to Google at all and no cookie is set. What it would record is anonymous: the page you are on, which controls you press, how far down you scroll, the distance and the filters you chose, and the airport code the page worked out for you. It never records a tail number, a callsign, or the place name you typed.
    • The only cookie is the analytics one, and it exists only if you accepted. Your answer itself is remembered in your browser's local storage, not in a cookie — setting a cookie to record a cookie decision would be its own small joke.
    • You can change your answer at any time with the Cookie settings button in the footer. Rejecting after accepting reloads the page so that counting genuinely stops.
    • What leaves your machine while the page is open. Every request goes to this domain — your address reaches this site's server, as it does for any page you open — and this site's own server passes the request on, so your browser asks no other site for anything, whether or not you accept cookies. Four things travel that way: a request for the aircraft inside your fence every twenty seconds or so, passed to the flight feed adsb.lol and carrying the centre and the distance you chose; a lookup of each callsign inside your fence, passed to adsbdb.com, which holds the route for a callsign; the place name you search for, or a position you asked the browser to find, passed to OpenStreetMap's Nominatim and Photon lookups to be turned into a place; and the map squares and the photographs of each type, passed to OpenStreetMap and to Wikipedia. Those services are asked by this site's server and are not given your address. The flight feed is under the Open Database License, the map squares under OpenStreetMap's own, and each photograph under the licence named beside it.
    • Aircraft data is about aircraft, not about people. The feed carries no passenger information, and none is available from it.
    • Notifications are sent by your own browser, from your own machine, and only if you pressed the button that asks for permission. Declining them changes nothing else on the page.
    • To contact anyone about this site, or to ask what is held about you: the answer is nothing, and the way to prove it is to clear this site's data in your browser. Questions can be raised through the contact form on nodejavascript.com.
